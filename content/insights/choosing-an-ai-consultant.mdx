Every AI consulting pitch sounds the same from the outside. Everybody says they start with your business, everybody says they build what you need, everybody has a deck with the same four logos on it. Sitting across the table, you have no way to tell one from another.

Six questions get genuinely different answers. We wrote them down because we would want a client to ask us all six, and because two of them are ones we have watched firms dodge.

## 1. Have you run the thing you are selling me?

There is a difference between a firm that has built AI operations for clients and a firm that has built them for itself. The second kind has been on the wrong end of its own mistakes.

Ask what they use internally, every day, that they did not buy. Ask what broke and what they did about it. A consultant who has only ever installed software for other people has never had to live with a bad decision for eighteen months.

We built and ran automation across our own businesses before we took on a single client. That was not a strategy, it was need, but it is the reason we can tell you what a given build will actually cost you in attention after it goes live.

## 2. Under what circumstances would you tell me not to do this?

Ask it exactly that way and listen to how long the pause is.

A firm that cannot describe a scenario where it walks away is a firm whose recommendation you cannot use, because the recommendation was already decided before the meeting. Every assessment we deliver can end with a recommendation to stop, and when it does, the client keeps the document and we are done.

If the answer you get is a version of "we would scope it smaller", that is not the same thing. Smaller is still a sale.

## 3. What do I own when this is over?

The question behind the question is what happens if you fire them.

You want documentation you can read without a special tool, accounts in your company's name rather than the consultant's, and no license that has to keep being paid for the work to keep running. Ask whether you could hand the whole thing to another firm next year. Ask whether it works with more than one AI vendor, or whether you are buying a dependency on the one they happen to resell.

A consultant whose work only makes sense while they are still being paid has built themselves a subscription, and you are it.

## 4. Who is in the loop when this thing acts?

The nightmare version of AI in a business is not that it stops working. It is that it goes on working, confidently, in the wrong direction, and nobody notices for a month.

Ask what the system is allowed to do without a person. Ask where the approval step sits, and what happens if somebody is on vacation. The honest answer starts narrow: it drafts, it proposes, it flags, and a person presses the button. You widen that over time as you watch it get things right, and widening it should be your call rather than a default somebody set at install.

## 5. How will I find out when it breaks?

It will break. A vendor changes an export format, an account expires, a login gets rotated by IT, and something quietly stops.

The difference between firms is whether the failure announces itself or hides. Ask specifically: when this stops working at 3am on a Sunday, who knows, and how? If the answer is that you would notice the output missing, the answer is that you would not notice for a week.

## 6. How is this priced, and what changes the number?

You want a fixed price per phase, given before you commit to that phase, with the previous phase already delivered so you know what you are buying. That structure lets you stop after any phase with something working in your hands.

Be suspicious of a single large number covering months of undefined work, and be equally suspicious of an hourly arrangement with no ceiling. Ask what would make the number go up. A firm that has scoped this kind of work before can list the drivers immediately: how much of the business is in scope, how many systems have to be mapped, how many people have to approve, and what state your data is in.

## What not to weigh heavily

Which AI model they use matters far less than they will suggest. The models change every few months and any competent build can move between them. What does not change is whether your process was understood before anything got built.

Case study counts do not mean much either, and neither does a list of industries served. Ask about one engagement in detail instead. How long it took, what they got wrong first, what the client does differently now. A firm that can walk you through a real one at that level of detail has actually done the work.

## The shortest version

If you only get to ask two, ask the second and the third. When would you tell me no, and what do I own at the end. Everything else you can work out from how somebody answers those.
